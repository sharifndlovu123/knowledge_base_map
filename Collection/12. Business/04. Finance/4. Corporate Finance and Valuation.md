# 04. Corporate Finance and Valuation

The maths of deciding where money goes, what it costs, and what a business or project is worth.

> **Not from the library.** General reference material written to fill a gap in the books in this section.

Builds on [[1. Reading the Numbers|Reading the Numbers]] and [[2. Cash Allocation|Cash Allocation]]; the bookkeeping behind the inputs is in [[3. Accounting Basics|Accounting Basics]].

## At a glance

| Framework | In one line | Use it when |
|---|---|---|
| Time value of money | A rand today beats a rand later | Comparing cash flows at different dates |
| NPV / IRR / payback | Judge a project by discounted cash flow | Choosing between investments |
| WACC | Blended cost of debt and equity | Setting the discount rate |
| CAPM | Cost of equity = risk-free + beta x premium | Estimating required equity return |
| Capital structure | Mix of debt and equity | Deciding how to fund |
| Valuation methods | DCF, multiples, asset-based | Pricing a business |
| Unit economics | CAC, LTV, contribution margin, break-even | Testing whether growth pays |
| Runway and burn | Cash / monthly net burn | Startups and loss-making periods |
| Funding options | Bootstrap, debt, equity, grants | Raising money |
| Sensitivity analysis | Vary assumptions, watch the answer | Any forecast |

## Time value of money
```
Future value   FV = PV x (1 + r)^n
Present value  PV = FV / (1 + r)^n
```
- Example: R100 000 at 10% for 3 years: FV = 100 000 x 1.331 = R133 100.
- Receiving R133 100 in 3 years at 10% is worth R100 000 today.
- Annuity (equal payments each period): PV = P x [1 - (1 + r)^-n] / r.
- Real vs nominal: inflation erodes value; use consistent rates (both nominal or both real).
- Mistake: adding cash flows from different years without discounting.

## NPV, IRR, payback
```
NPV = sum of [ CF_t / (1 + r)^t ]  - initial investment
```
- Accept if NPV > 0 (value created at the required rate).
- IRR: the discount rate that makes NPV = 0. Accept if IRR > required return.
- Payback: years until cumulative cash flow recovers the investment. Simple, ignores timing within and after payback.

| Item | Year 0 | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cash flow (R) | -100 000 | 40 000 | 50 000 | 60 000 |
| Discounted at 10% | -100 000 | 36 364 | 41 322 | 45 079 |

- NPV = 36 364 + 41 322 + 45 079 - 100 000 = R22 765, positive, so accept.
- Payback: cumulative 40 000, 90 000, 150 000; recovered in year 3 (about 2.2 years).
- IRR for this project: the rate where NPV = 0, about 21.6% (above the 10% required return, so accept).
- Mistakes: IRR with irregular/multiple sign changes gives odd answers; IRR ignores project scale (rank by NPV when capital is not binding); payback alone ignores later cash.

## Cost of capital (WACC)
```
WACC = E/V x Re + D/V x Rd x (1 - tax rate)
```
- E = equity value, D = debt, V = D + E; Re = cost of equity, Rd = pre-tax cost of debt.
- Example: 60% equity at 16%, 40% debt at 11%, tax 27%: 0.6 x 16% + 0.4 x 11% x 0.73 = 9.6% + 3.2% = 12.8%.
- Use WACC to discount project cash flows with that business's risk.
- Interest is tax deductible, hence the (1 - tax) factor on debt.
- Mistake: using one company-wide rate for a much riskier project.

## CAPM basics
```
Re = Rf + Beta x (Rm - Rf)
```
- Rf risk-free rate (government bond yield), Beta sensitivity to the market, (Rm - Rf) equity risk premium.
- Example: Rf 9%, beta 1.2, premium 5%: Re = 9% + 6% = 15%.
- Beta above 1 means more volatile than the market.
- Small private firms: add a size/illiquidity premium by judgement; CAPM is a starting point, not a measurement.

## Capital structure
- Debt is cheaper (tax shield, lenders rank ahead) but adds fixed obligations and bankruptcy risk.
- Equity is costlier but flexible, with no repayments.
- Leverage amplifies returns both ways.
- Checks: interest cover (EBIT / interest), debt to EBITDA, ability to service debt in a bad quarter.
- Rule of thumb: match funding to the asset (short-term working capital with short-term finance, long-lived assets with long-term).
- Mistake: borrowing against optimistic forecasts for a business with lumpy revenue.

## Valuation methods

| Method | How | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| DCF | Discount forecast free cash flow + terminal value | Based on fundamentals | Very sensitive to assumptions |
| Multiples | Apply comparable ratios (EV/EBITDA, P/E, EV/revenue) | Quick, market-anchored | Comparables may be poor |
| Asset-based | Net assets at fair value | Good for asset-heavy or failing firms | Ignores earning power and goodwill |

**DCF**
```
Terminal value (Gordon growth) = FCF_next / (r - g)
Enterprise value = sum of discounted FCF + discounted terminal value
Equity value = Enterprise value - net debt
```
- Example: FCF R1m next year, growing 3% forever, r = 12%: value = 1m / 0.09 = R11.1m.
- Terminal value often exceeds 60% of the total, so scrutinise g and r.

**Multiples**
- Example: EBITDA R2m, comparable businesses sell at about 5x: EV = R10m; subtract net debt R1m for equity R9m.
- Small private companies typically trade at a discount to listed peers (size, liquidity).

**Asset-based**: fair value of assets minus liabilities; useful as a floor.
- Cross-check two methods; the range is more honest than one number.

## Unit economics
```
Contribution margin per unit = Price - Variable cost
CAC  = Sales and marketing spend / New customers
LTV  = Average margin per period x Lifetime in periods   (or margin / churn rate)
Break-even units = Fixed costs / Contribution margin per unit
```
- Example: subscription R500/month, variable cost R150, so margin R350; churn 5% monthly: lifetime 20 months; LTV = R7 000.
- CAC R2 000: LTV:CAC = 3.5; CAC payback = 2 000 / 350 = 5.7 months.
- Common benchmark: LTV:CAC of about 3 or more and payback within roughly 12 months (rule of thumb, varies by sector).
- Break-even: fixed costs R70 000/month, margin R350: 200 customers.
- Use contribution margin, not revenue, in LTV; include onboarding and support costs in CAC.
- Mistake: counting blended CAC while paid channels are far costlier than organic.

## Runway and burn
```
Net burn = Cash out - Cash in (per month)
Runway (months) = Cash balance / Net burn
```
- Example: R600 000 cash, net burn R75 000: 8 months.
- Start the next raise or cost cuts at 9-12 months of runway, not 3.
- Track gross burn (total spend) and net burn separately.
- Mistake: forgetting lumpy annual costs and tax payments in the burn.

## Funding options

| Source | Cost | Control | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bootstrapping | Lowest, slower growth | Full | Customer revenue and owner savings |
| Debt (bank, asset finance, invoice discounting) | Interest + covenants/security | Kept | Needs reliable cash flow, often personal sureties for small firms |
| Equity (angels, VC) | Dilution | Shared | Fits high-growth, high-risk models |
| Grants / development funding | Often free, with conditions | Kept | Slow, competitive, reporting heavy |

- Match source to stage and risk profile.
- Dilution maths: raise R1m for 20% implies post-money R5m, pre-money R4m.
- South African grant and funding bodies change often; check the current programme terms before relying on one (not covered here).

## Sensitivity analysis
- Change one assumption at a time (price, volume, growth, discount rate) and record the effect on NPV/profit.
- Scenario analysis: best, base, worst with coherent assumptions.
- Example (same project as above): base NPV R22 765 at 10%. At a 15% discount rate NPV falls to about R12 041 (still positive). If the Year 2 and 3 cash flows come in 30% lower (35 000 and 42 000), NPV at 10% is about -R3 156, so the project is sensitive to those cash flows. Both together would give about -R11 100.
- Break-even assumption: find the value at which NPV = 0 and ask how plausible it is.
- Mistake: reporting a single point estimate as certainty.

## Recall

**Q: What does a positive NPV mean?**

??? note "Answer"
    The discounted cash inflows exceed the investment at the required rate, so the project creates value.

**Q: Why is debt's cost multiplied by (1 - tax rate) in WACC?**

??? note "Answer"
    Interest is tax deductible, so the after-tax cost to the company is lower.

**Q: Cash R600 000 and net burn R75 000 a month: runway?**

??? note "Answer"
    8 months.

**Q: What makes DCF risky as a method?**

??? note "Answer"
    The terminal value is a large share of the total and depends heavily on the growth and discount rate assumptions.
